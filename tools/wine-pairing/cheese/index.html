Why these wines work with cheese

Cheese and wine are an old partnership, but they are trickier together than tradition suggests. Strong cheese coats the palate with fat and salt and can flatten a delicate wine, while a big, tannic red can taste metallic against a creamy rind. The rule of thumb here is a wine with more age than the cheese: young, fresh cheeses suit young, fresh wines, and mature, nutty cheeses suit wines that have developed savoury depth of their own. A fresh goat’s cheese with a Sancerre is the classic case, the acidity in the wine matching the tang of the cheese. Soft, bloomy cheeses such as Brie welcome Champagne, whose bubbles cut through the richness, or a light Beaujolais. Hard, aged cheeses, Comté, Parmesan or a mature Cheddar, stand up to structured reds with some years on them: a Rioja, a Barolo, a Brunello from Montalcino, a mature Bordeaux. Blue cheese is where sweetness comes in, and the old pairings exist for a reason: Sauternes with Roquefort, Port with Stilton, the sugar balancing the salt and the intensity matching the intensity. A Tokaji Aszú, a PX or a Madeira does a similar job. Washed-rind cheeses, pungent and sticky, are easiest with an aromatic white such as Gewurztraminer. On a mixed board, a dry sherry or a sparkling wine is the most forgiving companion.
