When should I drink vintage Port?

Vintage Port is bottled young and unfiltered after about two years in wood, so almost all of its ageing happens in your cellar.

Why it needs time

Vintage Port is declared only in years the shipper judges outstanding, spends around two years in large wood, and is then bottled without filtration. Fermentation is stopped part way with grape spirit, leaving a wine of about twenty per cent alcohol with substantial residual sugar and dense tannin extracted by treading in granite lagares or their robotic modern equivalents. All of that matures slowly in bottle, which is why the window opens at fifteen years after the vintage and runs to forty. Single-quinta Ports, usually made in years that are not generally declared, are often ready sooner.

Opened too early

Young Vintage Port is a mouthful of blackcurrant and blackberry jam, liquorice and black pepper, with the spirit still noticeable as raw heat on the finish and tannin that grips hard. It is impressive but disjointed: sweetness, alcohol and tannin each pull in their own direction.

In the window

Over the years the colour fades from purple-black to ruby and then to tawny at the rim, a heavy crust forms in the bottle, and the spirit and sugar fuse with the fruit. Mature Port tastes of dried figs, plums, violets, cocoa, spice and cedar, with a smooth, warming finish. Well-stored bottles from strong declarations can still be drinking well beyond the closing figure.

Frequently asked questions

How long does vintage Port last once opened?

Not long. Unlike tawny, which has already been exposed to air in barrel, a mature Vintage Port is at its best on the day it is opened and fades over two or three days. Younger bottles hold up a little better. Recork any leftovers, keep them somewhere cool, and plan to finish the bottle within a few days.

Does vintage Port need decanting?

Yes. Vintage Port is unfiltered and throws a heavy sediment, or crust. Stand the bottle upright for a day or more, then pour it slowly into a decanter in front of a light, stopping when sediment reaches the neck. Old corks can crumble, so a two-pronged opener or Port tongs can help. Young Port benefits from a few hours of air; very old bottles are best served soon after decanting.

What is the difference between vintage Port and LBV?

Late Bottled Vintage also comes from a single year, but it spends four to six years in wood before bottling and is usually filtered, so it is ready to drink on release and does not need long cellaring. Vintage Port spends only around two years in wood and does almost all of its ageing in bottle. Unfiltered LBVs exist and can improve for a few years.
