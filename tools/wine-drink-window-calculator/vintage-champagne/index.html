Windows are broad guides for well-made, well-stored examples of each style — producer, vintage quality and storage all shift them.

What sets the window

Vintage Champagne must age for at least three years before release, most of it on the spent yeast from the second fermentation, and the better houses keep theirs considerably longer. That lees contact builds texture and a bready depth, while the high acidity of grapes grown near the northern limit of ripening is what lets the wine keep. Counted from the vintage, the window runs from eight to twenty years. A Pinot-led blend often develops faster than a Blanc de Blancs, which can stay taut for a long time, and wines from warm, ripe years tend to drink earlier than those from cool, high-acid ones.

Opened too early

Opened soon after release, vintage Champagne can feel more like a statement of acidity than a finished wine: lemon, green apple and chalk, a sharp, almost austere finish, and a mousse that is still a little aggressive. The lees character is there but in the background, as fresh bread rather than toast. What it lacks is time after disgorgement, when the wine and the dosage knit together.

In the window

With further bottle age the bubbles become finer and creamier, and the aromas move to brioche, toasted nuts, honey and baked apple, and eventually to mushroom and coffee. The acidity is still there but carries the richness rather than cutting across it. At this stage it is as much a wine for the table as an aperitif.

Frequently asked questions

How long can you keep vintage Champagne?

A good vintage Champagne will usually drink well for around twenty years from the harvest if stored cool and dark, and prestige cuvées from strong years can go further. Light and warmth damage sparkling wine quickly, so keep bottles away from both, and do not leave them in the fridge for months, where cold, dry air and vibration do them no good.

What does the disgorgement date on Champagne mean?

Disgorgement is when the yeast sediment is removed from the bottle and the dosage added before the final cork goes in. Some producers print the date on the back label. It matters because the wine keeps changing afterwards: a bottle disgorged recently tastes fresher and tighter than one of the same vintage disgorged years earlier. The window here is counted from the vintage, but the disgorgement date helps you place a particular bottle within it.
