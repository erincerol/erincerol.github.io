How often should I water a snake plant?

A snake plant stores water in its thick leaves and can go weeks without a drink, which is why more of them die from kindness than neglect.

Every 14–21 days
A starting point, not a rule — check the soil before each watering and let the top few centimetres dry out first.

Always confirm with the soil: water when the top 2–5 cm is dry, whatever the calendar says.

Light

Sansevieria, now classed as Dracaena trifasciata, tolerates dim corners, but it barely grows there and uses very little water, which is exactly when overwatering catches people out. In bright, indirect light, or even some direct sun, it grows steadily, and the calculator’s summer figure near a window is every 11–17 days.

Pot and soil

Use a gritty cactus or succulent mix, a pot only slightly wider than the root mass, and ideally terracotta, which lets the soil dry from the sides. Snake plants are happy crowded and tall ones tip over easily, so a heavy clay pot helps on both counts. Water onto the soil, not into the centre of the rosette, where water can sit and rot the crown.

Through the year

In medium light the calculator starts at every 14–21 days in spring and summer, 18–27 in autumn and 22–34 in winter. In low light in winter that becomes 29–44 days, and many growers water only once a month or less through the coldest weeks. Push a wooden skewer to the bottom of the pot: if it comes out damp, wait.

Too much or too little

Overwatering shows at the base first. Leaves turn soft and mushy where they meet the soil, yellow from the bottom up, and fall over or pull out with no resistance. By then the rhizome is often rotting, and the healthy upper sections of leaf are better saved as cuttings. Underwatering is rare and mild: leaves wrinkle, curl lengthways or develop dry brown tips, and firm up again after watering.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know when my snake plant needs water?

Wait for the soil to be dry all the way down, not only at the surface. A skewer or chopstick pushed to the bottom of the pot should come out clean and dry, and the pot should feel noticeably light. Leaves that are starting to look slightly thin or wrinkled also say it is time. Firm, upright leaves and damp soil mean leave it alone.

Why is my snake plant falling over?

Leaves that flop outward from the base are usually a sign of rot from too much water, especially if the base feels soft or smells. Check the roots: brown, mushy ones need cutting away and the plant repotting into dry, gritty mix. Leaves that lean but stay firm are more often reaching for light, and a brighter spot fixes them.
